## 1. Food systems — 2 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-FOOD_SYSTEMS`, FAO, “Agri-food systems”.

### Claim A — `CLM-FOOD_SYSTEMS-A`

Claim: a food system encompasses interconnected production, processing, distribution, consumption and disposal processes.

Exact support: FAO defines agri-food systems as all interconnected activities and actors involved in getting food from field to fork, encompassing agricultural production, processing, distribution, consumption and waste management.

Proposed locator: FAO, “Evaluating agri-food systems”, opening paragraph.

Assessment: direct, nearly verbatim support. “Waste management” is more precise than “disposal” because food systems include loss, waste prevention and recovery pathways as well as disposal.

### Claim B — `CLM-FOOD_SYSTEMS-B`

Claim: food systems include people, activities, investments and decisions connected with food production and delivery.

Exact support: FAO's definition includes activities and actors from production to consumption and waste management; its page explains that economic, social and environmental factors shape how food reaches people and that population growth, urbanization, wealth and consumption patterns influence system performance.

Proposed locator: FAO, “Evaluating agri-food systems”, opening paragraph and paragraph on system pressures.

Assessment: direct support. The exact phrase “investments and decisions” is not fully enumerated in the short FAO page; add a more detailed FAO food-systems framework if the Claim intends to list investment and policy decision-making as distinct system components.

## 2. Disaster risk reduction — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-DISASTER_RISK_REDUCTION_BASICS`, UNDRR, Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030.

### Claim A — `CLM-DISASTER_RISK_REDUCTION_BASICS-A`

Claim: disaster risk reduction aims to prevent new and reduce existing risk before a hazardous event occurs.

Exact support: Sendai Framework paragraph 17 defines disaster risk reduction as preventing new and reducing existing disaster risk through integrated and inclusive measures that prevent/reduce hazard exposure and vulnerability, increase preparedness and strengthen resilience.

Proposed locator: Sendai Framework, paragraph 17, definition of “Disaster risk reduction”.

Assessment: direct support. Preserve the full framework's inclusion of preparedness and resilience; the Claim's “before the hazardous event” phrasing is a simplified summary, not the whole definition.

## 5. Forestry — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-FORESTRY_BASICS`, FAO, Sustainable Forest Management.

### Claim C — `CLM-FORESTRY_BASICS-C`

Claim: forest-management objectives and measures depend on forest type, local conditions, law and stakeholder interests.

Exact support:
- FAO defines forest management as planning and implementing practices for stewardship and use of forests to meet environmental, economic, social and cultural objectives.
- FAO states that a precise global definition of sustainable forest management is impractical because forest types, conditions and socioeconomic contexts vary.
- The seven thematic elements of the UNFF reference framework allow forest owners and stakeholders to define sustainable forest management in specific countries and locations, including management objectives and how forests should be managed.
- FAO describes SFM as dynamic and multifaceted and says it adapts to changing local and national conditions.

Proposed locator: FAO, “Sustainable forest management”, “The concept of SFM”, “Defining SFM at national and local levels”, and “A multidimensional and dynamic concept”.

Assessment: direct support for all stated context factors. For actual forestry operations, add applicable national forest law, protected-area rules, tenure and forest-management plan.

## 6. Environmental policy — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-ENVIRONMENTAL_POLICY_BASICS`, UNEP, Environmental Rights and Governance.

### Claim C — `CLM-ENVIRONMENTAL_POLICY_BASICS-C`

Claim: concrete environmental requirements depend on jurisdiction, legal regime, time and subject matter; general principles cannot automatically be converted into local legal instructions.

Exact support:
- UNEP, *Environmental Rule of Law: Tracking Progress and Charting Future Directions* (2023), §1.3.1 “Fair, Clear, and Implementable Environmental Laws”, states that laws must be clear and implementable and adapted to national context, including institutional, cultural and economic context; laws also need to keep pace with technological developments.
- Chapter 2, “Institutions”, explains that national, regional and local institutions can have different jurisdiction over environmental law implementation/enforcement, and that sectoral bodies (finance, education, natural resources, fisheries, agriculture) can be involved.
- The report describes environmental regimes as overlapping and depending on health, agriculture, finance, land use, energy, infrastructure, national security, extractives and tax.

Proposed locator: UNEP 2023 report, §1.3.1, page 20; Chapter 2, §2.1.1 “Identifying Institutions Involved in Environmental Rule of Law”; Chapter 2 sections on multi-level and sectoral institutions.

Assessment: direct support. The current UNEP topic page is not the specific source passage; add the 2023 report as a separate Source/Evidence Use. This is a strong boundary Claim, not legal advice for any specific jurisdiction.

## 7. Water resources — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-WATER_RESOURCES_BASICS`, U.S. Geological Survey, Water Resources Mission Area.

### Claim B — `CLM_WATER_RESOURCES_BASICS_B`

Claim: assessment of water resources considers quantity and distribution as well as physical, chemical and biological characteristics.

Exact support:
- USGS “Water Resources Mission Area” describes the mission as improving understanding of water quantity, quality, availability and distribution in surface water and groundwater systems.
- USGS “Water Quality” defines water quality as suitability for a particular use based on selected physical, chemical and biological characteristics.
- USGS Fact Sheet 027-01, “A Primer on Water Quality”, gives examples of physical/chemical/biological characteristics and explains that suitability depends on designated uses.

Proposed locator: USGS Water Resources Mission Area, opening overview; USGS Water Quality, “What is water quality?”; USGS Fact Sheet 027-01, “What do we mean by water quality?” and “How is water quality measured?”.

Assessment: direct support. The canonical Source record covers water quantity/distribution but not the full quality-characteristics definition; add the specific USGS Water Quality source and fact sheet.

## 8. Matter and chemistry — 2 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-MATTER_BASICS`, OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §1.2 “Phases and Classification of Matter”.

### Claim B — `CLM-MATTER_BASICS-B`

Claim: a chemical element is defined by atoms with the same number of protons in the nucleus.

Exact support: OpenStax Chemistry 2e §1.2 defines an element as a pure substance that cannot be broken down by chemical changes; its linked atomic structure section explains atomic number as the number of protons in the nucleus, which identifies the element. A direct atomic-number locator is required in addition to §1.2.

Proposed locator: OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §1.2 “Phases and Classification of Matter”, “Pure Substances”; add §2.3 “Atomic Structure and Symbolism”, “Atomic Number”.

Assessment: direct support when both sections are linked. §1.2 alone does not state the proton-count definition in the exact form used by the Claim.

### Claim C — `CLM-MATTER_BASICS-C`

Claim: a mixture contains more than one chemical substance and can have variable composition.

Exact support: OpenStax Chemistry 2e §1.2 “Classifying Matter” states that mixtures contain two or more types of matter that can be present in varying amounts and can be separated by physical changes.

Proposed locator: OpenStax Chemistry 2e, §1.2 “Classifying Matter”, paragraph beginning “A mixture is composed…”.

Assessment: direct, nearly verbatim support. These are foundational chemistry definitions; the high-consequence machine flag is overinclusive for this use unless the material is later used for hazardous-chemical handling.

## 9. Volcanology — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-VOLCANOLOGY_BASICS`, USGS Volcano Hazards Program.

### Claim C — `CLM-VOLCANOLOGY_BASICS-C`

Claim: volcano monitoring uses geophysical, geochemical and visual data.

Exact support:
- USGS “Comprehensive monitoring provides timely warnings of volcano reawakening”, lines 33–40, says monitoring should include earthquakes, ground movement, volcanic gas, rock chemistry, water chemistry and remote satellite analysis, collected continuously or near-real-time.
- Lines 46–51 describe instrument data, seismic, tilt, GPS, gas, ground deformation and satellite imagery used to assess magma/fluid movement and hazards.

Proposed locator: USGS Volcano Hazards Program, “Comprehensive monitoring provides timely warnings of volcano reawakening”, paragraphs 2–4.

Assessment: direct support. Preserve the requirement for multiple observations and continuous/near-real-time monitoring; a single visual observation is not a sufficient volcano hazard assessment.

## 10. Cryptography — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-CRYPTOGRAPHY_BASICS`, NIST Cryptographic Standards and Guidelines.

### Claim C — `CLM-CRYPTOGRAPHY_BASICS-C`

Claim: cryptographic mechanisms may provide confidentiality, integrity and authentication depending on design.

Exact support:
- NIST SP 800-175B Rev. 1, abstract, says the document provides guidance for using cryptography and NIST standards to protect digitized information during transmission and storage.
- Keywords include identity authentication, confidentiality, digital signatures, encryption, integrity, key establishment and message authentication.
- NIST Cryptographic Standards and Guidelines page lists block ciphers, digital signatures, hash functions, key management, message authentication codes and other cryptographic mechanisms.

Proposed locator: NIST SP 800-175B Rev. 1, Abstract and Keywords; NIST Cryptographic Standards and Guidelines, “Crypto Standards and Guidelines Activities”.

Assessment: direct support for the security properties and the design-dependent nature of mechanisms. Preserve the distinction between confidentiality, integrity and authentication: no single cryptographic primitive necessarily provides all three, and implementation/key-management errors can invalidate intended properties.

## 11. Finance — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-FINANCE_BASICS`, Investor.gov, “Investing Basics”.

### Claim C — `CLM-FINANCE_BASICS-C`

Claim: valuation of a financial instrument depends on its characteristics, terms and the model used.

Exact support checked:
- Investor.gov “Investing Basics” is a general investor education page; it does not directly set out valuation models or how instrument terms affect valuation.
- IFRS 13 defines fair value measurement and requires selection of valuation techniques appropriate to the circumstances and available data; it describes market, cost and income approaches.
- IFRS 13 educational material states that the choice of technique depends on available information, market conditions, investment horizon/type, investee lifecycle, business nature and industry.
- For financial instruments, the standard's market approach includes matrix pricing for debt securities and the income approach includes present-value and option-pricing models.

Proposed locator: IFRS 13, paragraphs 61–63 and Appendix B5–B11; IFRS educational material, “Approaches to valuation”, paragraphs 17–20. Add IFRS 13 as a separate Source/Evidence Use and narrow the Claim to fair-value measurement under the stated accounting framework if that is the intended context.

Assessment: partial support from the currently linked Investor.gov source; direct support from IFRS 13 for fair-value measurement. The current EU/source chain is insufficient and must not be treated as a general investment valuation recommendation.

Disposition: HOLD.

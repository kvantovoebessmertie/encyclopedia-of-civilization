## 1. Environmental health — 2 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-ENVIRONMENTAL_HEALTH_BASICS`, WHO, Environmental health, https://www.who.int/health-topics/environmental-health

### Claim B — `CLM-ENVIRONMENTAL_HEALTH_BASICS-B`

Claim: environmental health risk assessment uses exposure information and relationships between exposure and health outcomes.

Exact support checked:
- WHO Environmental health, “Overview”, lines 86–89, identifies environmental determinants and health impacts, but does not describe the exposure-assessment or exposure-response method.
- WHO ECO methods page describes comparative risk assessment as using population exposure and a comprehensive analysis of how exposure translates into health outcomes; the population attributable fraction combines population exposure and the exposure-response relationship.
- EPA, “Conducting a Human Health Risk Assessment”, describes dose-response and exposure assessment as separate steps, with exposure assessment estimating magnitude, frequency and duration and risk characterization comparing exposure with expected effects.

Assessment: the Claim is methodologically correct, but the currently linked WHO topic overview is too broad to support the method. Add the WHO methods or EPA risk-assessment source explicitly and preserve a direct locator. Do not imply that the WHO overview itself supplies the full method.

Proposed locator: WHO ECO, “Methods”, comparative risk assessment paragraph; EPA, “Conducting a Human Health Risk Assessment”, “Step 2: Dose Response”, “Step 3: Exposure Assessment” and “Step 4: Risk Characterization”.

Disposition: HOLD pending an additional Source/EU and exact locators.

### Claim C — `CLM-ENVIRONMENTAL_HEALTH_BASICS-C`

Claim: environmental health requires cross-sector analysis because risks connect transport, energy, agriculture, cities and other systems.

Exact support:
- WHO Environmental health, “Overview”, lines 88–89, explicitly names health, energy, industry, agriculture and transport sectors and states that cross-sectoral collaboration is crucial.
- The same section explains indirect effects through food production, migration, economic instability and social inequalities.
- The page does not name cities in the exact cross-sector list, although urban health and sustainable urban environments are listed as related topics.

Assessment: direct support for the main cross-sector proposition. “Cities” is a reasonable application of the urban-health/transport/energy context but needs a city-health or urban-environment source if retained as a specific sector.

Proposed locator: WHO Environmental health, “Overview”, paragraphs 2–3; related topic “Urban health”.

Disposition: HOLD pending a city-specific locator/source if that component is retained.

## 2. Disaster behavioral health — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-DISASTER_BEHAVIORAL_HEALTH_BASICS`, SAMHSA, “Disaster Preparedness, Response, Recovery, and Mitigation”, https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/disaster-preparedness

### Claim A — `CLM-DISASTER_BEHAVIORAL_HEALTH_BASICS-A`

Claim: disaster behavioral health addresses mental, emotional and substance-use needs of people and communities affected by disasters.

Exact support: SAMHSA section “What is Disaster Behavioral Health (DBH)?” defines DBH as the understanding and provision of mental, emotional and substance-use services and interventions for people and communities impacted by disasters.

Proposed locator: SAMHSA, “What is Disaster Behavioral Health (DBH)?”, definition paragraph.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim B — `CLM-DISASTER_BEHAVIORAL_HEALTH_BASICS-B`

Claim: disaster behavioral-health activities can support preparedness, response, recovery and mitigation across different hazard types.

Exact support: SAMHSA states that DBH expertise and coordination supports partners as they prepare for, respond to, recover from and mitigate behavioral-health effects of weather/environmental disasters, public-health emergencies, extreme heat and climate-related emergencies. It separately lists integration across all aspects of disaster mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery.

Proposed locator: SAMHSA, opening paragraph after the DBH definition; bullet “Partnering with governmental and non-governmental partners”; bullet on climate and other hazards.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim C — `CLM-DISASTER_BEHAVIORAL_HEALTH_BASICS-C`

Claim: post-disaster behavioral-health interventions should account for the recovery phase and the affected community's conditions and needs.

Exact support: SAMHSA says services and interventions are delivered across preparedness, response, recovery and mitigation; it identifies at-risk populations, chronic behavioral-health conditions, social determinants of health, disaster resilience, trauma-informed services and local/community partners.

Proposed locator: SAMHSA, “What is Disaster Behavioral Health (DBH)?”, definition and following coordination/at-risk-population bullets.

Assessment: direct support for phase and community-context dependence. The SAMHSA page could not be opened directly by the web retrieval layer (403), but its official search-indexed content exposed the exact heading and text. Reconfirm by a human reviewer before locator entry.

Disposition: all three HOLD until exact heading/paragraph locators are entered and qualified behavioral-health review is complete.

## 3. History of medicine — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-HISTORY_OF_MEDICINE`, NLM MeSH, “History of Medicine”, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/68006666

### Claim A — `CLM-HISTORY_OF_MEDICINE-A`

Claim: history of medicine studies past events and the development of medicine as a field of knowledge and practice.

Exact support: NLM MeSH descriptor “History of Medicine” defines it as the study of past events and development in the field of medicine.

Proposed locator: NLM MeSH, “History of Medicine”, Scope Note.

Assessment: direct, concise definitional support.

### Claim B — `CLM-HISTORY_OF_MEDICINE-B`

Claim: the boundaries and content of medical practice have changed over time and require historical context.

Exact support: NLM Collection Development Guidelines, “History of Medicine”, “Discussion”, says that interpretive and analytical historical literature requires a broad interpretation of medical thought and practice and recognition that the boundaries of medicine change over time; it also emphasizes social, political, economic and religious factors.

Proposed locator: NLM, “Collection Development Guidelines — History of Medicine”, “Discussion”, paragraph 1.

Assessment: direct support, but the canonical MeSH Source record is not the Bookshelf Guidelines page. Add the NLM Bookshelf item as a distinct Source rather than treating the MeSH descriptor as though it contained this passage.

### Claim C — `CLM-HISTORY_OF_MEDICINE-C`

Claim: reconstruction of medical history uses both primary documents and interpretive literature, and their roles should be distinguished.

Exact support:
- NLM Collection Development Guidelines, “History of Medicine”, “Discussion”, distinguishes secondary literature used for interpretive, analytical and biographical studies.
- NLM Collection Development Guidelines, “Manuscripts”, states that manuscripts are important primary-source materials in the scholarly biomedical record.
- NLM Archives and Modern Manuscripts page describes unpublished historical records and examples such as correspondence, diaries, photographs, laboratory notebooks, patient records, speeches and lectures.

Proposed locators: NLM “History of Medicine”, “Discussion”; NLM “Manuscripts”, opening paragraph; NLM “About the Archives & Modern Manuscripts Collection”, “What are Archives and Modern Manuscripts?”.

Assessment: direct support for the distinction, but not from the currently linked MeSH descriptor. Add the Bookshelf “History of Medicine” and “Manuscripts” pages as separate Source records/Evidence Uses.

Disposition: all three HOLD pending the NLM source chain being extended and exact locators recorded.

## 4. Scientific publishing — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-SCIENTIFIC_PUBLISHING_BASICS`, ICMJE, “Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors”, https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html

### Claim A — `CLM-SCIENTIFIC_PUBLISHING_BASICS-A`

Claim: scientific publication is part of the process of presenting research results to the scientific community.

Exact support checked: ICMJE page “Why Authorship Matters” discusses authorship credit, accountability for published work and the need for contributorship policies, but it does not explicitly define scientific publication as the communication of research results to the scientific community.

Assessment: source-scope gap. ICMJE is relevant to medical journal authorship but is not a direct definition of scholarly communication/publication. Add a direct source on scholarly communication or research dissemination.

Proposed locator: no adequate locator in the current ICMJE page for the complete Claim. Candidate additional source: a scholarly communication/academic publishing reference defining publication as dissemination of research findings.

Disposition: HOLD; current EU wording is generic and the linked source does not directly establish the proposition.

### Claim B — `CLM-SCIENTIFIC_PUBLISHING_BASICS-B`

Claim: authorship is linked not only to credit but also to responsibility and accountability for published work.

Exact support: ICMJE, “Why Authorship Matters”, states that authorship confers credit and implies responsibility and accountability for published work. “Who Is an Author?” sets four criteria, including agreement to be accountable for all aspects and ensuring that accuracy/integrity questions are investigated and resolved.

Proposed locator: ICMJE, Section II.A.1 “Why Authorship Matters” and Section II.A.2 “Who Is an Author?”, criteria 1–4.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim C — `CLM-SCIENTIFIC_PUBLISHING_BASICS-C`

Claim: roles of authors and other contributors should be distinguishable so contribution and responsibility are not conflated.

Exact support: ICMJE “Why Authorship Matters” explains that authorship alone does not communicate what contributions qualified a person as an author and encourages contributorship policies. “Non-Author Contributors” describes contributions that should be acknowledged but do not alone qualify for authorship.

Proposed locator: ICMJE, Section II.A.1, paragraphs 2–3; Section II.A.3 “Non-Author Contributors”.

Assessment: direct support within medical-journal publishing. Do not automatically generalize ICMJE's medical-journal recommendations as binding rules for all disciplines or all publication venues.

Disposition: all three HOLD; add a direct source for Claim A and record exact ICMJE locators for B/C.

## 5. Social policy — 1 Claim

### Claim A — `CLM-SOCIAL_POLICY_BASICS-A`

Claim: social policy includes measures intended to protect people and families against social risks and support well-being.

Exact support:

- The linked ILO Source concerns social protection for forcibly displaced persons, which is a narrower application rather than a general definition of social policy.

Proposed locator: OECD “Social policy”, opening paragraph and “Key messages — Social spending aims to support individuals facing challenging circumstances”.

Assessment: direct support from OECD. Keep the ILO source as a contextual example only; do not describe the displaced-persons project page as a general social-protection standard.

Disposition: HOLD until the OECD locator is recorded and the narrower ILO source role is made explicit.

Proposed locator: OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e, §4.1 “Types of Tissues”, opening paragraph and “The Four Types of Tissues”.

Assessment: direct support from OpenStax. Do not rely on the inaccessible NIGMS URL as the sole source; verify its current redirect/availability.

Disposition: HOLD until the OpenStax locator is entered and the NIGMS Source is checked.

## 7. Wastewater — 1 Claim

Canonical Sources: `SRC-WASTEWATER_BASICS` (U.S. EPA, Wastewater 101: The Fundamentals) and `SRC-WHO-WASTEWATER-SAFE-USE-2006` (WHO Guidelines for safe use of wastewater, excreta and greywater).

### Claim A — `CLM-WASTEWATER_BASICS-A`

Claim: wastewater management includes collection and treatment before water is returned to the hydrological cycle.

Exact support:
- EPA “Wastewater 101” transcript, pp. 2–3, lines 43–50 and 101–112, explains wastewater as used water collected through sewerage and the purpose of treatment to remove undesirable constituents.
- EPA transcript, pp. 4–5, lines 146–166, describes physical separation and biological treatment processes.
- WHO Guidelines describe safe wastewater use and associated health/environment risk management, but do not themselves establish the full municipal collection-to-hydrological-cycle pathway.

Proposed locator: EPA “Wastewater 101: The Fundamentals”, transcript pp. 2–5, especially lines 43–50, 101–112 and 146–166. WHO guidance should be used only for the safe-use/risk-management component.

Assessment: direct support from EPA. The Claim is broad and should not imply all treated wastewater is immediately or universally returned to the hydrological cycle; treatment/discharge/reuse pathways depend on the system and applicable requirements.

Disposition: HOLD until the EPA locator is entered and the process boundary is retained.

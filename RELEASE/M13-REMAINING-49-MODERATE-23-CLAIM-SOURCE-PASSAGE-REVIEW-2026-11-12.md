## 2. Accounting, finance and administrative law — 5 Claims

### Accounting — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: IFRS Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting.

- CLM-ACCOUNTING_BASICS-A: The IFRS Conceptual Framework describes the objective of general-purpose financial reporting and the information it provides about an entity's economic resources, claims and changes in resources/claims. IAS 1 specifies the structure and components of financial statements. This supports the Claim at the financial-accounting/reporting level, but not every form of management or tax accounting.
- CLM-ACCOUNTING_BASICS-B: IAS 1 requires a structured set of statements, including financial position, financial performance, changes in equity, cash flows and notes. Direct support for structured presentation to users.
- CLM-ACCOUNTING_BASICS-C: IAS 8 governs selection and changes in accounting policies, changes in accounting estimates and correction of errors; the Conceptual Framework describes measurement bases and presentation. Direct support for dependence on applicable standards and circumstances. Do not generalize IFRS requirements to entities using another reporting framework.

Proposed locators:
- IFRS Conceptual Framework, Chapter 1 “The objective of general purpose financial reporting”; Chapter 3 “Financial statements and the reporting entity”.
- IAS 1, section “Complete set of financial statements”.
- IAS 8, sections “Accounting policies”, “Changes in accounting estimates” and “Errors”.

### Finance — 1 Claim

- CLM-FINANCE_BASICS-A: OpenStax Principles of Finance 2e §7.1 explains the time value of money and how the timing of cash receipts/payments changes their value; §7.3 uses timelines to organize cash flows over periods and discounts future cash flows to present value. Direct support for the basic proposition that financial decisions involve cash flows over time. This is a foundational concept, not personal investment advice.

Proposed locators: OpenStax Principles of Finance 2e, §7.1 “Now versus Later Concepts” and §7.3 “Methods for Solving Time Value of Money Problems”.

### Administrative law — 1 Claim

- CLM-ADMINISTRATIVE_LAW_BASICS-A: Cornell Law School's Wex definition states that administrative law governs the creation and operation of administrative agencies, including their powers, rules and legal relationships with other government bodies and the public. OECD Public Integrity Handbook, §4.2.2, describes domestic administrative law as defining the rights and duties of public administrations in their relations with the public. Direct support. Cornell's account is U.S.-oriented; the OECD source provides broader comparative context, but actual agency powers remain jurisdiction-specific.

Proposed locators: Cornell Wex, “Administrative law”, opening definition; OECD Public Integrity Handbook, §4.2.2 “Legal and regulatory frameworks and strategies” / subsection “Administrative law”.

## 3. Comparative and constitutional systems — 4 Claims

### Comparative law — 1 Claim

- CLM-COMPARATIVE_LAW_BASICS-A: Emory and Duke law-library research guides define comparative law as a method of comparing two or more legal systems, traditions, institutions and rules, rather than a separate body of substantive law. This directly supports the Claim. The canonical Cornell Wex “Legal systems” page is not a sufficiently precise definition of comparative law; the law-library guide should be added as a separate Source/Evidence Use after authorization.

Proposed locator: Emory University School of Law Library, “Foreign and Comparative Law Basics”, subsection “What is Comparative Law?”; Duke University School of Law, “Foreign & Comparative Law”, §I “Introduction”.

### Constitutional systems — 3 Claims

- CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-A: OpenStax Introduction to Political Science §9.2 describes presidential systems as having a stronger separation between executive and legislative branches. It supports institutional independence. The wording “elected separately” should be read as independently selected from the legislature, not as a universal claim that every presidential system uses direct popular election.
- CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-B: OpenStax §9.2 explains that parliamentary systems select the executive through the legislature; the prime minister is part of both executive and legislative branches. Direct support for the general pattern, while preserving variation between countries.
- CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-C: OpenStax §10.5 states that semi-presidential systems divide executive power between a president and prime minister, both of whom have political power. Direct support; the precise division of authority varies by constitution and political practice.

Proposed locators: OpenStax Introduction to Political Science, §9.2 “What Is the Difference between Parliamentary and Presidential Systems?” and §10.5 “Semi-Presidential Regimes”.

## 4. Behavioral economics and construction project management — 2 Claims

- CLM-BEHAVIORAL_ECONOMICS_BASICS-A: The 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences press release says Daniel Kahneman integrated insights from psychological research into economic science, particularly human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty; it also describes experimental economics. Direct support for the broad definition. Do not imply that one prize or one researcher exhausts the field.

  Proposed locator: NobelPrize.org, 2002 Economic Sciences Prize press release, subsection “Psychological and experimental economics”.

- CLM-CONSTRUCTION_PROJECT_MANAGEMENT_BASICS-C: The canonical Whole Building Design Guide landing page does not directly establish the stated dependency on project scale, contract, participants and jurisdiction. UK Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, regulations 4 and 12, provide a jurisdiction-specific example: clients must make suitable arrangements for project management, including sufficient time/resources; the construction phase plan must set out health/safety arrangements and be reviewed as the project changes. This supports project-specific tailoring but is not evidence of a universal global rule. Keep the Claim as a boundary statement and do not convert it into UK legal advice.

  Proposed locator: UK CDM Regulations 2015, regulations 4(1)–(5) and 12(1)–(4). Add a separate UK legal Source only if the Claim explicitly intends this example.

## 5. Diplomacy, human rights and international law — 6 Claims

### Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations — 2 Claims

- CLM-DIPLOMACY_BASICS-A: The UN Treaty Collection records the Convention as concluded in Vienna on 18 April 1961 and entering into force on 24 April 1964. Direct support.
- CLM-DIPLOMACY_BASICS-B: The Convention's preamble and Articles 2–3 establish the framework for diplomatic relations, including the principle that diplomatic relations and permanent missions are established by mutual consent and the functions of a diplomatic mission. Direct support. Preserve the fact that the Convention is a treaty with parties and obligations; do not imply that every state relationship is governed identically without checking treaty status and other applicable law.

Proposed locators: UN Treaty Collection, Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, treaty metadata and text, Articles 2–3.

### Universal Declaration of Human Rights — 2 Claims

- CLM-HUMAN_RIGHTS_BASICS-A: The UN states that the General Assembly proclaimed the UDHR on 10 December 1948. Direct support.
- CLM-HUMAN_RIGHTS_BASICS-B: The UDHR text sets out a common standard of fundamental rights and freedoms in 30 articles. Direct support for what the Declaration says. Preserve its legal status: it is a declaration, not itself a treaty; the legal force of particular rights may also arise from treaties, customary international law and domestic law.

Proposed locators: UN, “Universal Declaration of Human Rights”, opening historical paragraph and the Preamble/Articles 1–30.

### International law — 1 Claim

- CLM-INTERNATIONAL_LAW_BASICS-A: The UN describes international law as defining State responsibilities in their conduct with each other and treatment of individuals within State boundaries. Cornell Wex also describes rules governing States, international organizations and individuals. Together these support the broad statement, but the canonical UN Charter landing page alone does not define the full range of subjects. The status and legal personality of non-State actors vary by context; avoid suggesting that all actors have identical legal capacity.

Proposed locators: UN, “Uphold International Law”, subsection “What is International Law?”; Cornell Wex, “International law”, opening definition. Add these as direct Sources/Evidence Uses under authorization.

## 6. International relations, labour relations, public administration and public finance — 5 Claims

- CLM-INTERNATIONAL_RELATIONS_BASICS-A: The UN identifies the Charter as the founding document of the United Nations, signed on 26 June 1945 and in force from 24 October 1945. Direct support. The Charter is also an international treaty and an instrument of international law.

  Proposed locator: UN, “UN Charter”, opening paragraph.

- CLM-LABOR_RELATIONS_BASICS-A: ILO Convention No. 154, Article 2, defines collective bargaining as negotiations between an employer/employers' organization and workers' organization(s) for determining working conditions and terms of employment and/or regulating relations. Direct support. The Claim's “one of the mechanisms” wording is appropriately bounded.

  Proposed locator: ILO, “What is collective bargaining?”, quotation of Convention No. 154, Article 2.

- CLM-PUBLIC_ADMINISTRATION_BASICS-C: OECD Public Integrity Handbook, §2.1, explains that public-sector responsibilities are allocated across institutions and levels of government and depend on institutional and jurisdictional setup. Cornell Wex explains that administrative agencies receive powers through statutes and that their authority and relationships are shaped by law. Direct support for the boundary proposition. This is not a statement of any particular government's current powers.

  Proposed locators: OECD Public Integrity Handbook, “Responsibilities”, §2.1 “Why responsibilities?”; Cornell Wex, “Administrative law”, “Structure of Agencies”.

- CLM-PUBLIC_FINANCE_BASICS-A: IMF explains that fiscal policy uses government taxation, spending and borrowing to influence economic activity and allocate public resources. Direct support.

  Proposed locator: IMF Finance & Development, “Fiscal Policy: Taking and Giving Away”, “How fiscal policy works”.

- CLM-PUBLIC_FINANCE_BASICS-C: IMF and OECD material explains the relationship between taxes as a source of government revenue and public expenditure financed by those revenues. Direct support at the general level. Actual revenue and spending structures differ by jurisdiction and level of government.

  Proposed locators: IMF, “Fiscal Policies”, overview; OECD, “Tax policy”, opening paragraph and key messages on revenue financing public spending.

## 7. Public policy and taxation — 2 Claims

- CLM-PUBLIC_POLICY_BASICS-A: OECD Government at a Glance 2025, section “Public policy evaluation”, describes evaluation as an element of policy design and implementation, including institutional arrangements and use of evaluation results. Direct support. The Claim is correctly phrased as “may be embedded”; it should not imply that all governments consistently evaluate every policy.

  Proposed locator: OECD, Government at a Glance 2025, “Public policy evaluation”, opening paragraphs and section on evaluation across the policy cycle.

- CLM-TAXATION_BASICS-A: OECD states that tax policy seeks to secure revenue for government programmes and public services, while tax rules and design influence economic incentives and distributional outcomes. IMF describes tax policy as a means of raising revenue to meet spending needs. Direct support. Preserve jurisdictional and temporal boundaries: actual tax liabilities, rates and procedures depend on current domestic law.

  Proposed locators: OECD, “Tax policy”, opening paragraph and “Taxes seek to raise revenue”; IMF, “Tax Policy”, “Purpose and objectives”.

## 2. Source-passage findings

### Modeling — 1 Claim

NIBIB's “Computational Modeling” page defines computational modeling as using computers to simulate and study complex systems using mathematics, physics and computer science. It explains that models use variables to simulate systems under different conditions and distinguishes mechanistic from data-driven models.

- Claim C's boundary that model results must be interpreted within assumptions, variables and conditions is consistent with the source's description of model inputs and simulated outputs.
- The NIBIB page is about computational modeling in biomedical contexts; it is not a universal validation standard. The Claim is general, and the Evidence Use has no locator.

### Political economy — 2 Claims

IMF Finance & Development, “The Political Economy of Economic Policy” (June 2020), discusses concentrated and diffuse interests, institutions shaping which groups are decisive, and the effects of political institutions on policy speed and scope. It explains how political incentives and interest-group organization shape policy outcomes.

- Claim B is directly supported at the general level: interests and institutions influence policy selection and implementation through political rules and influence distribution.
- Claim C is plausible and context-bounded, but the cited article is stronger on political incentives shaping economic policy than on economic change causing changes in political behaviour. Partial support; avoid asserting universal causal direction or magnitude.
- The EU descriptions include some contextual qualification but no reproducible section locator.

### Project management — 2 Claims

PMI's “What Is Project Management?” defines project management as applying knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet requirements, and describes planning, organizing and executing tasks to deliver products/services/results. It lists scope, deliverables, risk and communication. PMI also says approaches should be tailored to project needs, distinguishing predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches. The PMI standard summary identifies requirements, stakeholder needs and competing constraints such as scope, quality, schedule, budget, resources and risk.

- Claim B is supported by the PMI description and standard process framing.
- Claim C is directly supported by the tailored predictive/adaptive/hybrid approaches and explicit statement that different projects require different approaches.
- The EU records lack exact locators; E remains 1.

### Queueing theory — 3 Claims

MIT OpenCourseWare's “Queues: Theory and Applications” describes modelling and analysis of queueing systems, including single/multi-server queues, queue networks, Markovian queues and applications in communications, manufacturing, computing, call centres, services and transportation. A standard queueing-theory chapter summary describes the basic model as input/arrival process, service mechanism and queue discipline.

- Claim A is a basic definition and is consistent with the MIT course's subject.
- Claim B's factors—arrival, service, number of servers and queue discipline—are part of the basic queue model, but the linked MIT course landing page does not itself preserve a claim-specific lecture locator.
- Claim C's approximation and assumption-dependence is methodologically correct, but the MIT course overview does not directly establish all stated assumptions. A model-specific lecture or textbook passage is needed.
- All three remain HOLD pending exact lecture/source locators and source-scope checks.

### Research design — 3 Claims

NIH Library's “Overview of Study Design” training covers biomedical study designs, observational and experimental designs, descriptive and analytic studies, and advantages/disadvantages of analytic designs.

- Claim A connects design choice to the research question and the observations/comparisons available. The NIH training is relevant, but the page does not directly state the entire question-to-design logic.
- Claim B's observational/experimental distinction is directly covered by the NIH training.
- Claim C's need to assess advantages, limitations and applicability is consistent with the NIH page's explicit attention to design advantages/disadvantages and different design types.
- This is a biomedical training overview; do not generalize it as a complete methods standard for every discipline.

### Research integrity — 2 Claims

The National Academies' “Fostering Integrity in Research” recommends effective, responsible and transparent data handling and emphasizes openness, data management and record keeping. It supports the need for transparent records and responsible processes.

- Claim B's clear responsibilities and transparent records are consistent with the report. Its specific correction/concern-handling mechanism needs a more direct report chapter locator.
- Claim C makes a valid epistemic distinction: integrity practices do not prove the truth of an individual claim. The cited report is relevant to research integrity but does not, from the reviewed passage, directly establish this exact distinction. E remains 1 and additional epistemology/evidence source may be warranted.

### Science policy — 2 Claims

OECD's “Science policy” page says science informs policy responses, effective crisis response requires mobilising scientific capacity, and actors in science systems need to be connected through networks; it highlights the science-policy-public interface and cooperation mechanisms.

- Claim B is directly supported at the broad level of multiple actors, coordination and communication.
- Claim C's distinction between evidence informing policy and not determining value judgments or political decisions is an important boundary. OECD's page supports the “informing” side; the normative distinction is a reasoned boundary and should be paired with a direct policy-analysis source.
- The OECD page is a topic overview, not a claim-specific pinpoint.

### Scientific reproducibility — 3 Claims

National Academies, *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science* (2019), Chapter 3:
- defines reproducibility as consistent results using the same input data, computational steps, methods, code and analysis conditions;
- defines replicability as consistent results across studies answering the same question, each using its own data;
- explains that scientific knowledge develops through confirmatory research, examination of limits and repeated testing;
- states that measurement limitations and intrinsic variability create uncertainty, and that researchers should characterize and report uncertainty.

- Claim A is directly supported by the report's definitions. The source record points to the committee initiative page rather than the report itself, so canonical Source/EU needs a precise report locator.
- Claim B is directly supported by the report's treatment of uncertainty and measurement limitations.
- Claim C is directly supported by the report's account of confirmatory research and independent scrutiny, while the report also cautions that a failed replication does not automatically refute the original finding.
- The claim should preserve the report's nuance: replication is not a simplistic binary truth test.

### Seasons and orbits — 3 Claims

NASA Science, “Seasons on Earth, Mars, and Beyond,” says Earth's seasons result from axial tilt relative to the orbital plane; as Earth orbits the Sun, hemispheres tilt toward or away from the Sun. It also explains that tilt changes the amount of sunlight received through the year. NASA Space Place's educational explanation states that different parts of Earth receive the Sun's most direct rays during the year.

- Claim A is directly supported.
- Claim B is directly supported.
- Claim C is broadly supported by NASA's description of changing sunlight due to tilt, though the exact “angle of incidence” wording should be tied to a source passage explicitly discussing solar angle and day length.
- All three are basic astronomy Claims; no risk-bearing operational implication is intended.

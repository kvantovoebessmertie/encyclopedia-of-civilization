## 2. Material rights finding: OpenStax is not a safe default for this AI-assisted workflow

The current OpenStax 2023 *World History Volume 1, to 1500* notice states both:
- book-level CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 terms (including NonCommercial and ShareAlike); and
- the book may not be used for LLM training or otherwise ingested into LLM/generative-AI offerings without prior written permission.

Official notices:
- https://openstax.org/books/world-history-volume-1/pages/preface
- https://help.openstax.org/s/article/Licensing-information-of-OpenStax-textbooks

This is a distinct restriction from the ordinary CC BY-NC-SA attribution/reuse conditions. A citation or public URL is not itself permission for AI ingestion. The project should not use OpenStax as an affirmative evidence text in an AI-ingested or AI-assisted corpus/workflow unless written permission is obtained and the distribution model is separately cleared.

Conservative recommendation: do not use OpenStax §3.1, §2.3 or §1.2 as the proposed canonical Evidence Use basis for Package A at this stage. Preserve the existing @1 records as historical records; do not delete, overwrite or silently relabel them. This recommendation does not itself alter their current state.

### Claim A — early civilization characteristics

**A1. McMahon et al. (2023)**  
“Dense urbanism and economic multi-centrism at third-millennium BC Lagash,” *Antiquity* 97(393):596–615. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2023.36.  
Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/dense-urbanism-and-economic-multicentrism-at-thirdmillennium-bc-lagash/2D27165A470831B32B9B29C72EBEF8AD  
Pinpoints: Abstract; Introduction; discussion/conclusion on dense occupation, walled quarters and multiple production foci.  
Rights: publisher explicitly identifies article as CC BY 4.0.  
Role/limit: strong site-specific archaeological support for dense urban occupation and production specialization at Lagash; does not independently establish a universal early-civilization template or social stratification across civilizations.

**A2. Twiss et al. (2024)**  
“‘But some were more equal than others:’ Exploring inequality at Neolithic Çatalhöyük,” *PLOS ONE* 19(9):e0307067. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0307067.  
Publisher page: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0307067  
Pinpoints: Abstract; Results and Discussion; Conclusion.  
Rights: article text is distributed under the Creative Commons Attribution License; third-party material must be checked separately.  
Role/limit: important limiting/counterexample evidence. It finds differentiated assets and uneven distributions, but no evidence for institutionalized or lasting economic/social inequality. It should prevent the Encyclopedia from equating every material difference with a rigid hierarchy. Do not reuse the separately credited Fig. 1 photograph without its own permission.

**Assessment:** these two works offer complementary case evidence, but they do not fully establish every element of the existing broad Claim A. In particular, the existing Claim wording may imply wider generality than two site studies can prove. Do not silently rewrite Claim A; flag its scope for a separately authorized editorial decision.

### Claim B — agriculture, settlement and demography

**B1. Lesure et al. (2021)**  
“Large-Scale Patterns in the Agricultural Demographic Transition of Mesoamerica and Southwestern North America,” *American Antiquity* 86(3):593–612. DOI: 10.1017/aaq.2021.23.  
Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/largescale-patterns-in-the-agricultural-demographic-transition-of-mesoamerica-and-southwestern-north-america/31DD609E9D81DC72BFC9CB28D72E0F27  
Pinpoints: Abstract; “Spatial Structure”; “Temporal Structure”; “The Agricultural Demographic Transition in Two Macroregions”; “A Look at Other Macroregions”; Conclusions.  
Rights: publisher explicitly identifies article as CC BY 4.0.  
Role/limit: regional settlement/subsistence synthesis; describes prolonged, uneven demographic transitions. Not a universal global sequence.

**B2. Crema et al. (2024)**  
“Regional variations in the demographic response to the arrival of rice farming in prehistoric Japan,” *Antiquity* 98(401):1290–1305. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2024.146.  
Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/regional-variations-in-the-demographic-response-to-the-arrival-of-rice-farming-in-prehistoric-japan/7E6D28520A04B2F07DDD36908F291808  
Pinpoints: Abstract; Introduction; Results; Discussion; Conclusions.  
Rights: publisher explicitly identifies article as CC BY 4.0.  
Role/limit: settlement and radiocarbon evidence shows regionally different population-growth trajectories after rice farming arrived. Radiocarbon-date frequency is a proxy with calibration, sampling and chronology uncertainties, not a census. The article explicitly cautions that diffusion tempo alone does not explain demographic outcomes.

**B3. Neil et al. (2016)**  
“Isotopic evidence for residential mobility of farming communities during the transition to agriculture in Britain,” *Royal Society Open Science* 3(1):150522. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.150522.  
Publisher/repository record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736932/  
Pinpoints: Abstract; Introduction; Results and Discussion.  
Rights: repository/publisher record identifies CC BY 4.0.  
Role/limit: strontium and oxygen isotope evidence from an Early Neolithic Gloucestershire burial population is consistent with residential mobility; it is a useful counterexample to treating agriculture as synonymous with immediate permanent sedentism. It is a geographically specific sample.

**Assessment:** this is the strongest proposed alternative set for Claim B. Together the studies support regional variation and the need to avoid a simple universal causal narrative. They do not justify claiming that agriculture alone caused all settlement or demographic changes.

### Claim C — archaeological and written evidence

**C1. Hawkes et al. (2022)**  
“Grounding texts and theories of societal change,” *Antiquity* 96(387):611–627. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2022.28.  
Publisher page: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/grounding-texts-and-theories-of-societal-change/AE0B62DF8479F955D9D758FF8CB3547C  
Pinpoints: Abstract; Introduction; Results; Conclusions.  
Rights: publisher explicitly identifies article as CC BY 4.0.  
Role/limit: a case-specific example combining written land-grant charters with archaeological survey of 268 religious and residential sites in South Asia. Chronologically this concerns the mid-first millennium AD / Early Historic period; whether it is within the Encyclopedia's chosen meaning of “ancient societies” must be decided editorially. It is not evidence that all ancient societies had writing.

**Assessment:** useful as an example of combining documentary and archaeological evidence, but its period label needs editorial fit review. The Claim itself is a general methodological statement; one case study can illustrate it, not exhaust the full range of evidence types or societies.

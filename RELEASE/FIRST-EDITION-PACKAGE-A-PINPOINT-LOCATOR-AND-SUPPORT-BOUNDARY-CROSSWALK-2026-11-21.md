## 2. Claim A — early urban and social organization

### Open Book Project textbook locator

- *World History, Volume 1, to 1500*, Chapter 3, §3.1 “Early Civilizations”.
- Subsection: “Attributes of Early Civilizations”.
- Subsection: “The First Urban Societies”.
- Link: https://open-book-project.github.io/textbooks/social-science-textbooks/world_history_1/early-civilizations-and-urban-societies.html

Support: educational synthesis of early civilization features, including surplus, specialization, social hierarchies and urban settlement. This is adapted from OpenStax; do not count it as an independent source from OpenStax.

### Independent locator A1 — Lagash

McMahon et al. (2023), *Antiquity* 97(393):596–615, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2023.36.

- Publisher article abstract: identifies dense urban occupation, walled quarters and multiple foci of intensive industrial production.
- Introduction: early urbanism and Lagash as a case study.
- Results / discussion and conclusion: use for case-specific settlement density, neighbourhood structure and production organization.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/dense-urbanism-and-economic-multicentrism-at-thirdmillennium-bc-lagash/2D27165A470831B32B9B29C72EBEF8AD

Support boundary: Lagash is a specific Early Dynastic Mesopotamian site. It cannot alone establish a universal trait of all early civilizations.

### Independent locator A2 — broad review of early cities

Fernández-Götz & Smith (2024), *Annual Review of Anthropology* 53:231–247, DOI 10.1146/annurev-anthro-041222-094823.

- Abstract: diversity of premodern urbanization; cities as concentrations of people, institutions and activities.
- Review sections on definitions of cities, urban functions, methodological advances, and urban persistence/collapse.
- Link: https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-anthro-041222-094823

Support boundary: review-level synthesis, not a primary excavation report; use to contextualize diversity rather than as a single universal definition.

### Independent locator A3 — urban form and agroecology

Styring et al. (2022), *Journal of Agrarian Change* 22(4):831–854, DOI 10.1111/joac.12497.

- Abstract: relationship between cereal isotope values, settlement size and population size across northern Mesopotamia, the Aegean and south-west Germany.
- §4 “Assessing regional patterns in the relationship between cereal manuring intensity and urban form”.
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580239/

Support boundary: useful for the interaction of urban form, settlement density and production organization; not direct proof of social stratification.

### Independent locator A4 — social differentiation and hierarchy caution

Twiss et al. (2024), *PLOS ONE* 19(9):e0307067, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0307067.

- Abstract.
- Introduction: discussion of inequality and early agropastoralism.
- “Results and discussion” subsections on synchronic inequality and change through time.
- Conclusion/discussion: authors caution that measured dissimilarities are not automatically proof of institutionalized hierarchy.
- Link: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0307067

Rights note: article text is CC BY; Fig. 1 credits Jason Quinlan's photograph and the Çatalhöyük Research Project. Do not reuse that photograph under the article-level licence alone.

Support boundary: a case-specific counterweight to simple assumptions about inequality; do not claim Çatalhöyük demonstrates a permanent stratified hierarchy.

## 3. Claim B — agriculture, settlement and demographic variation

### Open Book Project textbook locator

- *World History, Volume 1, to 1500*, Chapter 2, §2.3 “The Neolithic Revolution”.
- Subsection: “The Development of Agriculture”.
- Subsection: “How Farming Changed the Human Experience”.
- Link: https://open-book-project.github.io/textbooks/social-science-textbooks/world_history_1/early-humans.html

Support: broad synthesis of regional emergence of agriculture and changes in human settlement/life. Adapted from OpenStax; not independent corroboration.

### Independent locator B1 — Mesoamerica and Southwest demographic transition

Lesure et al. (2021), *American Antiquity* 86(3):593–612, DOI 10.1017/aaq.2021.23.

- Abstract: two phases of rapid population growth and differing spatial/social consequences.
- “Spatial Structure” and “Temporal Structure”.
- “The Agricultural Demographic Transition in Two Macroregions”.
- “A Look at Other Macroregions”.
- “Conclusions”: long, uneven transitions; some regions lack a clear early rapid-growth phase.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/largescale-patterns-in-the-agricultural-demographic-transition-of-mesoamerica-and-southwestern-north-america/31DD609E9D81DC72BFC9CB28D72E0F27

Support boundary: strong regional case and settlement-data synthesis, not a universal global sequence.

### Independent locator B2 — prehistoric Japan

Crema et al. (2024), *Antiquity* 98(401):1290–1305, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2024.146.

- Abstract: settlement and radiocarbon evidence for regional variation in demographic trajectories after rice farming.
- Introduction: §§ on regional variation, tempo/mode of diffusion and episodes of return to forager subsistence.
- Results: comparison of radiocarbon summed probability distributions and settlement data across eight regions.
- Conclusions: regional differences and uncertainty about the mechanisms driving them.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/regional-variations-in-the-demographic-response-to-the-arrival-of-rice-farming-in-prehistoric-japan/7E6D28520A04B2F07DDD36908F291808

Support boundary: radiocarbon-date frequency is a proxy; article itself notes sampling, calibration, chronological and inferential uncertainty.

### Independent locator B3 — residential mobility in Britain

Neil et al. (2016), *Royal Society Open Science* 3(1):150522, DOI 10.1098/rsos.150522.

- Abstract.
- Introduction: competing models of sedentary versus mobile early farming communities.
- Methods/results: strontium and oxygen isotope analysis of tooth enamel from Early Neolithic Gloucestershire.
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736932/

Support boundary: case-specific evidence that early farming did not necessarily mean full sedentism.

### Independent locator B4 — demic versus cultural diffusion in Europe

Fort (2015), *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 12(106):20150166, DOI 10.1098/rsif.2015.0166.

- Abstract: analysis of dates from approximately 900 archaeological sites.
- Results/discussion: comparison of demic, cultural and mixed diffusion models across European regions.
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4424695/

Support boundary: supports differing mechanisms of agricultural spread; not itself a direct measurement of demographic or settlement consequences.

### Independent locator B5 — urban form and production strategy

Styring et al. (2022), *Journal of Agrarian Change* 22(4):831–854, DOI 10.1111/joac.12497.

- Abstract.
- §4 “Assessing regional patterns in the relationship between cereal manuring intensity and urban form”.
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580239/

Support boundary: comparative association between settlement size/urban form and agricultural strategy; avoid inferring simple one-way causation.

### Context-only locator B6 — crop diversity regions

Khoury et al. (2016), *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 283:20160792, DOI 10.1098/rspb.2016.0792.

- Introduction: definition of primary regions of crop diversity.
- Methods: geographical regions and crop assignments.
- Results: current food-supply and agricultural-production systems.
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920324/

Support boundary: current food systems and crop-diversity geography; not a direct source for prehistoric domestication chronology or ancient demographic causation.

### Citation-only locator B7 — Near Eastern origins

Zeder (2011), *Current Anthropology* 52(S4):S221–S235, DOI 10.1086/659307.

- Abstract.
- “A New Picture of Agricultural Origins in the Near East”.
- Link: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/659307

Rights boundary: publisher states © Wenner-Gren Foundation, all rights reserved, and provides a permissions route. “Free” access is not a reuse licence. Cite/link only; do not copy, bulk extract or AI-ingest absent permission and policy clearance.

## 4. Claim C — evidence about ancient societies

### Open Book Project textbook locator

- *World History, Volume 1, to 1500*, Chapter 1, §1.2 “Primary Sources”.
- Subsections: “Documentary Sources: Competing Narratives”; “Textual Sources: The Importance of Language”; “Hidden in History”.
- Link: https://open-book-project.github.io/textbooks/social-science-textbooks/world_history_1/understanding-the-past.html

Support: identifies written/documentary and non-textual/archaeological source types. Do not import the chapter's generalized hierarchy that privileges written evidence over oral or material sources.

### Independent locator C1 — texts and archaeological investigation used together

Hawkes et al. (2022), *Antiquity* 96(387):611–627, DOI 10.15184/aqy.2022.28.

- Abstract: textual record of land-grant charters combined with archaeological investigation and a survey of 268 religious and residential sites.
- Introduction: discussion of the relationship between textual and material approaches.
- Conclusions: archaeological contextualization of texts and integration of objects/texts in studying societal change.
- Link: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/grounding-texts-and-theories-of-societal-change/AE0B62DF8479F955D9D758FF8CB3547C

Support boundary: a documented early historic South Asian example; do not generalize it to cultures or periods without surviving writing.

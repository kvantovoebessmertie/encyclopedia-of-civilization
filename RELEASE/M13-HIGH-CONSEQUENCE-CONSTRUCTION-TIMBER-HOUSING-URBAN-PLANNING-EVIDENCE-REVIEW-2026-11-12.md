## 1. Construction — building science and performance

Canonical Source: `SRC-CONSTRUCTION_BASICS`, NIST, Building Science / Measurement Science for Building Systems Program.

### Claim A — `CLM-CONSTRUCTION_BASICS-A`

Claim: building performance is shaped by the interaction of building components, materials, systems and the surrounding environment over the service life.

Exact support:
- NIST Measurement Science for Building Systems Program, lines 134–138: building performance depends on interactions among components; performance cannot be defined by one metric and requires consideration of energy/water costs, IAQ, thermal comfort, lighting, acoustics and other factors.
- Lines 145–151: integrated test beds evaluate whole-building system interactions; performance is measured under actual weather conditions over time; ventilation and IAQ affect safety, comfort and performance.
- Lines 177–179: plumbing design and operation, flow rates, residence times and water quality interact over time.

Proposed locator: NIST, “Measurement Science for Building Systems Program”, “What is the problem?” lines 134–138; “Integrated Whole-Building Performance” lines 145–151; “Premise plumbing systems” lines 177–179.

Assessment: direct support. The NIST program page is more precise than the broad NIST Building Science landing page originally linked; add it as a separate Source/Evidence Use rather than silently substituting the URL.

### Claim C — `CLM-CONSTRUCTION_BASICS-C`

Claim: general building-science information does not replace project-specific calculations, applicable codes/standards and qualified technical inspection.

Exact support:
- NIST lines 137–142 and 151 state that measurement science and research inform industry-consensus standards, guidelines and specialized testing; they are not themselves a project-specific structural design approval.
- NIST's historical “Building research at the National Bureau of Standards” paper describes the research division as a technical resource and explicitly says it does not establish performance limits for particular applications.
- GSA Project Planning Guide lines 443–466 requires GSA building systems to meet P-100 performance standards and calls for fire-protection engineering and life-safety assessment to identify unacceptable risks.

Assessment: the boundary is prudent and consistent with the distinction between research information, standards and project-specific assessment. However, the current NIST Source does not itself state a universal legal rule requiring “qualified technical inspection”. Add applicable building-code, professional-engineering and inspection standards for the jurisdiction and building type.

Proposed locator: NIST measurement-science page lines 137–142; GSA Project Planning Guide, “Physical Plant and Structure”, “GSA’s Facilities Standards for the Public Buildings Service (P-100)” and “Fire Protection Engineering and Life Safety”.

Disposition: HOLD pending precise project/code/qualification boundary sources.

## 2. Construction project management — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-CONSTRUCTION_PROJECT_MANAGEMENT_BASICS`, Whole Building Design Guide / GSA Project Planning Guide.

### Claim A — `CLM-CONSTRUCTION_PROJECT_MANAGEMENT_BASICS-A`

Claim: construction project management coordinates goals, participants, time, cost, quality and risk.

Exact support:
- GSA Project Planning Guide, Introduction, lines 13–28, defines the Guide as outlining the project delivery process and the roles of team members, experts and intended users.
- Feasibility Study summary, lines 203–237, lists integrated project team, goals, roles/responsibilities, alternatives, program/cost implications, logistical risks, schedule and Project Management Plan.
- Project Management Plan section, lines 778–780, explicitly states that cost, quality and schedule are key components and that the PMP includes work plan, schedule, quality assurance, communications and controls.
- Lines 754–755 state procurement methods depend on project needs/complexity and that delivery choices balance quality, time, budget, risk and flexibility.

Proposed locator: GSA Project Planning Guide, “Feasibility Study Process”, “Project Management Plan” and “Procurement Method”.

Assessment: direct support for the Claim's elements in a federal capital-project context. The Guide is U.S.-specific and older; it is a process example, not a universal construction law or current project-management standard. A current GSA P-100/PMP guide or ISO 21502/PMI construction guidance should be added if the Claim is intended as globally normative.

Disposition: HOLD until the exact locator and jurisdiction/scope boundary are entered in EU.

## 3. Housing systems — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-HOUSING_SYSTEMS_BASICS`, UN-Habitat housing topic.

### Claim C — `CLM-HOUSING_SYSTEMS_BASICS-C`

Claim: adequate housing depends on affordability, accessibility, security, infrastructure, environmental quality and social/cultural context.

Exact support:
- Habitat Agenda (1996), paragraph 60, defines adequate shelter as including privacy, space, physical accessibility, security, tenure, structural stability/durability, lighting, heating, ventilation, water, sanitation, waste management, environmental quality, health-related factors, accessible location and affordability.
- The same paragraph states that adequacy is determined with the people concerned and varies by country according to cultural, social, environmental and economic factors.
- Paragraph 61 describes the responsibilities of governments and the roles of private sector, NGOs, communities and local authorities.

Proposed locator: Habitat Agenda, A/CONF.165/14, Chapter III, B. “Adequate shelter for all”, paragraph 60; paragraph 61 for institutional responsibilities.

Assessment: direct support. The linked UN-Habitat topic page is broad; the Habitat Agenda paragraph is the exact primary source. Add the Habitat Agenda as a separate Source/Evidence Use and do not treat a topic landing page as a substitute for the primary document.

Disposition: HOLD pending exact primary-source locator.

## 4. Timber engineering — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-TIMBER_ENGINEERING_BASICS`, USDA Forest Service Forest Products Laboratory, *Wood Handbook: Wood as an Engineering Material*, revised 2021 (FPL-GTR-282).

### Claim A — `CLM-TIMBER_ENGINEERING_BASICS-A`

Claim: timber structures depend on the mechanical properties and grading of wood, structural analysis and the design of connections.

Exact support:
- Chapter 5, “Mechanical properties of wood”: wood properties vary considerably; moisture, soil conditions, growing space, knots, slope of grain, manufacturing and service environments affect mechanical properties.
- Chapter 7, “Stress grades and design properties”: stress grades categorize wood by sorting criteria, engineering design properties and grade name.
- Chapter 8, “Fastenings”: structural strength/stability depends heavily on fastenings; joint design must account for strength along/across grain and dimensional changes.
- Chapter 9, “Structural analysis equations”: structural analysis mathematically models forces, deformations and stresses and is used in design.
- Chapter 17, “Use of wood in buildings and bridges”: describes building systems and engineered wood products used in buildings and transport structures.

Proposed locators: Wood Handbook, Chapters 5, 7, 8, 9 and 17.

Assessment: direct support for the core engineering relationships. This is technical background, not a substitute for a current national timber design code or product-specific approval.

### Claim B — `CLM-TIMBER_ENGINEERING_BASICS-B`

Claim: moisture content and environmental conditions affect wood dimensions and performance.

Exact support:
- Chapter 4, “Moisture relations and physical properties of wood”, explains that moisture exchange depends on relative humidity, temperature and the current water content of the wood; moisture relationships affect wood properties and performance.
- Chapter 13, “Drying and control of moisture content and dimensional changes”, states that correct drying, handling and storage minimize moisture changes in service and that moisture control can avoid major dimensional-change problems.

Proposed locators: Wood Handbook, Chapter 4, opening sections on wood-water relations; Chapter 13, “Moisture content control during transit, storage, and construction”.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim C — `CLM-TIMBER_ENGINEERING_BASICS-C`

Claim: safe long-term timber performance depends on design, connections, moisture control, fire/biodeterioration protection and quality control.

Exact support:
- Chapters 5, 7, 8 and 9 support material variability, stress grading, connection design and structural analysis.
- Chapter 13 covers moisture content and dimensional changes.
- Chapters 14–15 cover biodeterioration and wood preservatives.
- Chapter 18, “Fire safety of wood construction”, discusses building-code fire requirements, fire performance data, evaluation and flame-retardant treatments.
- Chapter 5 also discusses manufacturing and service environments and the effect of growth features.

Assessment: direct support for design, connection, moisture, biological and fire-performance dependencies. “Quality control” is only partially supported by stress grading and material variability; a separate grading, production quality-assurance or product-conformity standard is needed if the Claim intends a formal QA system.

Proposed locators: Wood Handbook, Chapters 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, 14, 15 and 18; add the applicable lumber grading/product standard for quality control.

Disposition: all three HOLD pending exact chapter locators and current jurisdiction-specific timber design standards.

## 5. Urban planning — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-URBAN_PLANNING_BASICS`, UN-Habitat, Urban Planning and Design topic. Additional exact source: UCLG/UN-Habitat/UNDP, *SDG Localization Module 2: Planning for change with the SDGs* (May 2024).

### Claim A — `CLM-URBAN_PLANNING_BASICS-A`

Claim: urban planning integrates land use, housing, transport, infrastructure and public spaces.

Exact support:
- 2024 Module 2, Chapter 2, lines 487–498: spatial planning considers population, infrastructure networks and natural resources; master planning includes land use, infrastructure, transportation and public spaces; land-use planning allocates residential, commercial, industrial and recreational areas.
- Lines 466–476 define urban/territorial planning as an economic, social, cultural and environmental decision-making process and a multidimensional process.

Proposed locator: Module 2, Chapter 2, “Urban and territorial planning as a multidimensional process: Strategic and spatial planning”, lines 466–498.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim B — `CLM-URBAN_PLANNING_BASICS-B`

Claim: planning scales and priorities depend on local territorial needs and governance arrangements.

Exact support:
- Module 2, Chapter 2, lines 463–465 says planning responds to precise territorial needs and that intervention scales differ by each country's governance arrangements, from transboundary/national through regional, metropolitan, city, municipal and neighborhood levels.
- Lines 481–484 explain that strategic planning reflects political priorities and may span different electoral terms.
- Chapter 1, lines 310–326, says local government competences, mandates and autonomy vary by country and that national-local links must be analyzed in context.

Proposed locator: Module 2, Chapter 2, lines 463–465 and 481–484; Chapter 1, lines 310–326.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim C — `CLM-URBAN_PLANNING_BASICS-C`

Claim: planning assessment must consider site/context constraints, infrastructure, regulatory and institutional arrangements.

Exact support:
- Module 2, Chapter 2, lines 463–466 links planning to territorial needs and governance arrangements.
- Lines 487–498 identifies population distribution, infrastructure, natural resources, land use, population density, accessibility, zoning, environmental considerations and community input.
- Lines 473 and 502–509 identify policy principles, institutional/participatory mechanisms, regulatory procedures and variation in national urban-policy arrangements.
- GSA Project Planning Guide lines 683–698 provides a project/site example: site investigation considers subsoil, contamination, urban design, expansion, demolition and relocation.

Assessment: direct support for the general context-dependent planning principle. A specific site decision still requires actual site investigations, local zoning/planning rules, hazard maps, utilities and legal requirements; a global planning guide cannot certify any individual site.

Proposed locators: Module 2, Chapter 2, lines 463–498 and 502–509; add site-specific studies and the applicable local planning code when used for a real site.

Disposition: all three HOLD pending the specific 2024 Module 2 Source/EU and exact locators.

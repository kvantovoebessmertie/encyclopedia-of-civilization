### 2.1 Early urbanism — broader synthesis for Claim A

**Manuel Fernández-Götz and Michael E. Smith (2024), “The Archaeology of Early Cities: ‘What Is the City but the People?’”** *Annual Review of Anthropology* 53:231–247. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-anthro-041222-094823.

The publisher page expressly states CC BY 4.0. The review describes premodern urbanization as more diverse than older general models suggest and organizes the field around definitions of cities, concentrations of people/institutions/activities, methods, and urban persistence and collapse.

Use: broad scholarly framing and a guard against treating a single urban form as universal. It is a review, not a primary archaeological case study.

Publisher: https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-anthro-041222-094823

### 2.2 Agriculture and demographic change in Mesoamerica / Southwest — strong support for Claim B

**Richard G. Lesure, R. J. Sinensky, Gregson Schachner, Thomas A. Wake and Katelyn J. Bishop (2021), “Large-Scale Patterns in the Agricultural Demographic Transition of Mesoamerica and Southwestern North America.”** *American Antiquity* 86(3):593–612. DOI: 10.1017/aaq.2021.23.

Cambridge expressly identifies CC BY 4.0. The study analyses published settlement and subsistence data and finds prolonged, two-phase demographic transitions in Mesoamerica and the Southwest. Early population growth was spatially limited and modest; later growth had a wider regional footprint and distinct social, economic and political consequences. The authors also note regions without a clear first rapid-growth phase.

Use: strong region-specific support for the proposition that agricultural change and demographic outcomes were associated but varied in timing, spatial scale and consequences. Avoid converting the regional model into a universal causal law.

Publisher: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/largescale-patterns-in-the-agricultural-demographic-transition-of-mesoamerica-and-southwestern-north-america/31DD609E9D81DC72BFC9CB28D72E0F27

### 2.3 Rice farming and regional demographic differences in prehistoric Japan — strong support for Claim B

**Enrico R. Crema, Simon Carrignon, Shinya Shoda and Chris J. Stevens (2024), “Regional variations in the demographic response to the arrival of rice farming in prehistoric Japan.”** *Antiquity* 98(401):1290–1305. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2024.146.

Cambridge expressly identifies CC BY 4.0. The study combines settlement and radiocarbon evidence and finds different regional trajectories following the arrival of rice farming. The article describes different rates and modes of dispersal, possible slowdowns, leapfrogging, and even returns to forager-based subsistence in some areas. Its results do not support a simple assumption that faster agricultural diffusion necessarily predicts higher population growth.

Use: particularly well matched to Claim B's qualification that direction and strength of the relationship differed by region and time. It also supplies a clear boundary: radiocarbon-date frequency is a demographic proxy with known inferential limits, not a direct census.

Publisher: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/regional-variations-in-the-demographic-response-to-the-arrival-of-rice-farming-in-prehistoric-japan/7E6D28520A04B2F07DDD36908F291808

### 2.4 Farming and residential mobility in Neolithic Britain — strong counterexample/limiting evidence for Claim B

**Samantha Neil, Jane Evans, Janet Montgomery and Chris Scarre (2016), “Isotopic evidence for residential mobility of farming communities during the transition to agriculture in Britain.”** *Royal Society Open Science* 3(1):150522. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.150522.

The PubMed Central record identifies the Royal Society CC BY 4.0 licence. Strontium and oxygen isotope analyses of tooth enamel from an Early Neolithic burial population in Gloucestershire are consistent with residential mobility among early farming communities, rather than complete settlement.

Use: a direct empirical limit on any assumption that the adoption of agriculture necessarily meant permanent settlement. It helps preserve the cautious wording of Claim B.

Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736932/

### 2.5 Urban form, settlement size and agricultural strategy across three regions — support for Claims A and B

**Amy K. Styring, Chris U. Carmona, Valasia Isaakidou, Angeliki Karathanou, Geoff K. Nicholls, Anaya Sarpaki and Amy Bogaard (2022), “Urban form and scale shaped the agroecology of early ‘cities’ in northern Mesopotamia, the Aegean and Central Europe.”** *Journal of Agrarian Change* 22(4):831–854. DOI: 10.1111/joac.12497.

The PubMed Central record identifies CC BY 4.0. The study uses cereal-grain nitrogen isotope values and settlement size to examine agricultural intensity and population size at Neolithic to Bronze/Iron Age sites in northern Mesopotamia, the Aegean and south-west Germany. It finds that urban form and settlement density shaped different agroecological trajectories: high-density urbanism in northern Mesopotamia and the Aegean was associated with agricultural extensification, whereas lower-density urbanism in south-west Germany allowed more intensive arable-land management.

Use: comparative support for the interaction of settlement form, agricultural organization and population aggregation; importantly, it demonstrates regional variation rather than a single universal pathway.

Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580239/

### 2.6 Regional diffusion models — supporting context for Claim B

**Joaquim Fort (2015), “Demic and cultural diffusion propagated the Neolithic transition across different regions of Europe.”** *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 12(106):20150166. DOI: 10.1098/rsif.2015.0166.

The PubMed Central record identifies CC BY 4.0. Using dates from approximately 900 archaeological sites, the study compares models of demographic and cultural diffusion. Its results indicate different dominant mechanisms across regions: cultural diffusion in Northern Europe, the Alpine region and west of the Black Sea, and demic diffusion in the Balkans and Central Europe.

Use: support for regional variation in how agricultural practices spread. It does not by itself quantify settlement or population effects.

Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4424695/

### 2.7 Textual and archaeological evidence used together — supporting example for Claim C

**Jason D. Hawkes, Riza Abbas, Anne Casile, Jaseera C.M., Coline Lefrancq and Sitaram Toraskar (2022), “Grounding texts and theories of societal change.”** *Antiquity* 96(387):611–627. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2022.28.

Cambridge expressly identifies CC BY 4.0. The article brings together a textual record of land-grant charters with archaeological investigation and a survey of 268 religious and residential sites in South Asia. It demonstrates a concrete case in which documentary/textual materials and archaeological/settlement evidence are jointly used to examine historical change.

Use: a case-specific methodological example for Claim C. The case concerns early historic South Asia, not every ancient society; it should not be presented as proof that written materials survive for all ancient cultures.

Publisher: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/grounding-texts-and-theories-of-societal-change/AE0B62DF8479F955D9D758FF8CB3547C

## 5. Evidence-quality recommendations (research only)

1. **Claim A:** the independent evidence base is now materially stronger. Pair a broad review of early urbanism with the specific Lagash case and the Çatalhöyük study's caution about equating inequality with institutionalized hierarchy. The claim remains broad; if its scope is later reconsidered, do so only under separate Claim-edit authorization.
2. **Claim B:** the strongest new evidence is Lesure et al. 2021 and Crema et al. 2024, with Neil et al. 2016 as a direct mobility counterexample. These studies make the claim's regional and temporal qualification empirically meaningful. Do not rely on Khoury et al. 2016 alone for prehistoric causation.
3. **Claim C:** Hawkes et al. 2022 is a useful independently licensed example of textual and archaeological evidence used together. The general claim can remain modest and conditional (“where they survive”); do not import the Open Book Project chapter's generalized ranking of written sources above oral or material evidence.

## 1. `CLM-GAME_THEORY_BASICS-A` — **D2 / E1 / B2**

**Claim:** Game theory formalizes strategic interactions in which one participant’s outcome depends on other participants’ actions.

**Source check:** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entry defines game theory as studying strategic interactions and outcomes tied to agents’ preferences (introductory paragraph, https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2014/entries/game-theory/). It further describes situations where an agent’s best action depends on expectations about others’ actions (section 1). The source directly supports the core concept. The stored EU is generic and does not identify either passage (E1).

**Assessment:** The claim conveys the core relational structure of strategic interaction rather than merely naming the field (D2). It does not mention preferences/payoffs, information, or the fact that formal models rely on assumptions (B2).

**Disposition:** Add a pinpoint to the definition and section 1. Consider a short note that the model’s predictions depend on how players, strategies, information, and preferences are represented.

## 2. `CLM-GAME_THEORY_BASICS-B` — **D2 / E1 / B3**

**Claim:** A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no participant has an incentive to change strategy unilaterally given the others’ strategies.

**Source check:** SEP section 2.5 (“Solution Concepts and Equilibria”) defines Nash equilibrium for a set of strategies and says no player could improve their payoff, given others’ strategies, by changing their own strategy (https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2014/entries/game-theory/, section 2.5). This is direct support, but the stored EU is still generic (E1).

**Assessment:** The claim captures the central unilateral-deviation criterion (D2). “Given the strategies of the others” is an important boundary; it does not imply that the equilibrium is unique, socially optimal, or fair (B3).

**Disposition:** Retain provisionally and add the section 2.5 locator. Ensure the adjacent Claim C makes the distinction between equilibrium and desirability clear.

## 3. `CLM-GAME_THEORY_BASICS-C` — **D2 / E1 / B3**

**Claim:** An equilibrium as a solution to a game model does not automatically mean the outcome is desirable or socially optimal.

**Source check:** SEP section 2.5 explains that multiple Nash equilibria may exist and that equilibrium alone can fail to select the intuitively sensible outcome; it explicitly discusses cases where an outcome may be socially superior but not a Nash equilibrium (paragraphs around lines 256–263 in the online entry). This supports the distinction between equilibrium and desirability. The EU provides no pinpoint (E1).

**Assessment:** The claim makes a useful distinction between a formal solution concept and a normative evaluation (D2). “Does not automatically” is appropriately bounded; it avoids saying equilibria can never be desirable or efficient (B3).

**Disposition:** Retain provisionally with a section 2.5 locator. The source is an archived Fall 2014 SEP edition; it remains conceptually relevant, but the editor should confirm whether a current edition is available before public release.

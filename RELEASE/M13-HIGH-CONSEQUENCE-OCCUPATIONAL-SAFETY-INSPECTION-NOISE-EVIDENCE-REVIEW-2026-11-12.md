## 1. Occupational safety — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-OCCUPATIONAL_SAFETY_BASICS`, ILO, Safety and Health at Work, https://www.ilo.org/safework/

### Claim A — `CLM-OCCUPATIONAL_SAFETY_BASICS-A`

Claim: occupational safety and health aims to prevent harm and create safe and healthy working conditions.

Exact support:
- ILO “Safety and health at work” states that occupational safety and health protects lives, prevents harm and ensures workers can carry out their jobs safely and with dignity.
- ILO recognizes a safe and healthy working environment as a fundamental principle and right at work.

Proposed locator: ILO, “Safety and health at work”, opening paragraph.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim B — `CLM-OCCUPATIONAL_SAFETY_BASICS-B`

Claim: occupational safety management includes risk identification/control, organizational processes and worker participation.

Exact support:
- ILO “Occupational Safety and Health Management Systems”, “A systems approach to manage occupational safety and health”, states that hazard identification, risk assessment and control are key principles.
- The same section says OSH management systems integrate into business planning and support worker participation in determining and implementing preventive measures.
- The page's “OSH Management System” section lists policy, organizing, planning and implementation, evaluation and improvement.

Proposed locator: ILO, “Occupational Safety and Health Management Systems”, paragraphs 1–4 and “OSH Management System”.

Assessment: direct support for all components.

### Claim C — `CLM-OCCUPATIONAL_SAFETY_BASICS-C`

Claim: workplace safety depends on national/sectoral requirements, conditions of a specific workplace and the nature of hazards.

Exact support:
- ILO states that enterprises must tailor OSH systems to their needs and means; “there is no one size fits all”.
- It says OSH requirements are pursuant to national laws and regulations and that many countries have national OSH strategies.
- The page distinguishes national, sectoral and workplace levels and identifies risks that emerge from specific work contexts.

Proposed locator: ILO OSH Management Systems, paragraphs 2–5 and “OSH Management System”.

Assessment: direct support for the contextual and jurisdictional boundary. Actual compliance instructions still require the applicable national law and sectoral standard.

## 2. Inspection — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-INSPECTION_BASICS`, OSHA, Field Operations Manual, Chapter 3 — Inspection Procedures, https://www.osha.gov/fom/chapter-3

### Claim A — `CLM-INSPECTION_BASICS-A`

Claim: inspection is a systematic check of an object, process or conditions against predetermined criteria.

Exact support:
- OSHA FOM Chapter 3, “General Inspection Procedures”, says effective inspections require judgment in identifying, evaluating and documenting safety/health conditions and practices.
- “Inspection Scope” distinguishes comprehensive and partial inspections and describes scope as limited to potentially hazardous areas, operations, conditions or practices.

Proposed locator: OSHA FOM Chapter 3, §§ 1 “General Inspection Procedures” and 3 “Inspection Scope”.

Assessment: partial support. The source describes workplace safety inspections, not a universal definition of every inspection type. Keep the Claim explicitly general/introductory or add a broader quality/technical inspection standard.

### Claim B — `CLM-INSPECTION_BASICS-B`

Claim: inspection results depend on scope, observation methods, criteria and quality of recorded data.

Exact support:
- OSHA FOM Chapter 3, “General Inspection Procedures”, identifies judgment in evaluation and documentation and says scope/detail vary by circumstances.
- “Inspection Preparation and Planning” requires review of relevant worksite/inspection history and hazard context.
- “Inspection Scope” distinguishes comprehensive/partial inspections and ties scope to the specific areas, operations, conditions or practices under review.
- The “Conduct of Inspection” and “Review of Records” sections describe walkarounds, interviews, photographs, records and other collected information.

Proposed locator: OSHA FOM Chapter 3, §§ 1–3, 4 “Conduct of Inspection”, and 6 “Review of Records”.

Assessment: direct support in the OSHA workplace-inspection context.

### Claim C — `CLM-INSPECTION_BASICS-C`

Claim: inspection can identify deviations or conditions requiring further assessment, but does not itself replace a specialized technical opinion.

Exact support:
- OSHA FOM Chapter 3, “Expert Assistance”, says the Area Director should arrange a specialist and/or specialized training when the need for expertise is identified; specialists may accompany inspectors or perform tasks separately.
- “General Inspection Procedures” emphasizes professional judgment and documented observations; a comprehensive inspection may still not inspect every potentially hazardous condition.

Proposed locator: OSHA FOM Chapter 3, “Expert Assistance” and “General Inspection Procedures”.

Assessment: direct support for the need to distinguish inspection findings from specialist expertise. Preserve the boundary: an inspection report is not automatically a design certification, structural assessment or other specialized technical conclusion.

## 3. Environmental noise — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-ACOUSTICS_BASICS`, WHO Regional Office for Europe, Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region (2018), https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789289053563

### Claim A — `CLM-ACOUSTICS_BASICS-A`

Claim: noise exposure can be considered an environmental factor affecting health and well-being.

Exact support:
- WHO guidelines executive summary says environmental noise is an important public-health issue with negative impacts on human health and well-being.
- WHO “Noise” overview lists sleep disturbance, cardiovascular/metabolic and cognitive effects, hearing impairment and annoyance.

Proposed locator: WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines, Executive Summary, “Overview”; WHO Europe, “Noise”, “Overview”.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim B — `CLM-ACOUSTICS_BASICS-B`

Claim: noise sources and levels differ by place, time and type of activity.

Exact support:
- WHO guidelines identify road traffic, railway, aircraft, wind turbine and leisure noise as distinct sources.
- The guideline framework uses long-term average exposure indicators Lden and Lnight, with separate recommendations for day-evening-night and night exposure.
- The WHO recommendations include source-specific exposure levels and distinguish transport, wind turbine and leisure sources.

Proposed locator: WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines, source-specific recommendations and the sections explaining Lden/Lnight and each noise source.

Assessment: direct support for differences by source and time. “Place” is only indirectly supported through source/context; add local exposure mapping or a source-specific noise assessment standard if spatial variation is intended as a distinct factual component.

### Claim C — `CLM-ACOUSTICS_BASICS-C`

Claim: noise recommendations depend on source, exposure time, context and the applicable regulatory system.

Exact support:
- WHO guideline recommendations differ for road, rail, aircraft, wind turbine and leisure noise and distinguish average exposure from night exposure.
- WHO says its European Region guidelines are intended to support policy action; the European Environmental Noise Directive (2002/49/EC) provides an EU framework for assessment and management of environmental noise.

Proposed locator: WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines, source-specific recommendations and Lden/Lnight definitions; WHO Executive Summary, “Overview”; the relevant local/EU noise legislation for the jurisdiction under discussion.

Assessment: partial support. Source and time dependence are direct. “Applicable regulatory system” is a jurisdictional boundary and cannot be inferred from WHO health guidance alone; EU Directive context is European and should not be universalized.

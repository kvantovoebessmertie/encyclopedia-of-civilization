## 1. Comparative civilizations — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: UNESCO Multimedia Archives, “History of Civilizations of Central Asia”.

### CLM-COMPARATIVE_CIVILIZATIONS_BASICS-A
Claim: “Comparative study of civilizations compares societies in their historical and cultural context without assuming a single universal trajectory of development.”

Exact support checked:
- The UNESCO resource describes a multi-volume history of Central Asian civilizations across different periods, from early civilization to the present.
- It is a regional historical synthesis and does not set out a comparative-civilization method or explicitly reject a universal trajectory.

Assessment: NOT DIRECTLY SUPPORTED by the current canonical Source. The source is relevant as a regional historical case, but it cannot support the methodological proposition. Add a scholarly comparative-history/civilization source that explicitly addresses contextual comparison and rejects unilinear or universal evolutionary models. Keep HOLD.

## 2. Comparative law — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: Cornell Law School Legal Information Institute, Wex, “Legal systems”.

### CLM-COMPARATIVE_LAW_BASICS-A
Claim: “Comparative law compares legal systems, institutions, rules and methods of application across different jurisdictions.”

Exact support:
- Cornell Wex explains that legal systems differ between jurisdictions and that the system used affects applicable sources of law, judicial roles, standards of evidence and procedures.
- The University of Michigan Law Library guide defines comparative law as the scholarly study of similarities and differences between legal systems of different jurisdictions.
- The guide notes that comparison may address legal traditions, institutions, rules and legal methods.

Assessment: DIRECT when the Michigan Law Library source is added as a distinct Source/Evidence Use; the current Cornell source is contextual support but does not itself define comparative law. Proposed locators: University of Michigan Law Library, “Foreign, Comparative, and International Law: Definitions”, subsection “Comparative Law”; Cornell Wex, “Legal systems”, opening paragraphs.

## 3. Computing — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: OpenStax, Introduction to Computer Science.

### CLM-COMPUTING_BASICS-A
Claim: “A computer system processes data according to defined instructions using hardware and software components.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §5.1 states that a computer system consists of hardware and software; hardware executes programs that are fed with data.
- The section explains that the CPU fetches, decodes and executes instructions and that programs and data are stored in digital form.

Assessment: DIRECT support. Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Computer Science §5.1, “Computer Systems”, “Data Storage Inside a Computer” and “Hardware Organization of Systems”.

### CLM-COMPUTING_BASICS-B
Claim: “An algorithm specifies a sequence of steps for solving a problem or transforming input data.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §2.1 defines an algorithm as a sequence of steps/instructions followed in a specific order to solve a problem.
- §3.1 defines an algorithm as a sequence of precise instructions operating on data; §3.2 explains that an algorithm takes input and computes a corresponding output.

Assessment: DIRECT support. Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Computer Science §§2.1, 3.1 and 3.2.

### CLM-COMPUTING_BASICS-C
Claim: “Data and instructions can be represented digitally in a form processed by a computing system.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §5.1 explains that programs and data are stored as sequences of 1s and 0s (bits/bytes).
- It describes program instructions encoded in binary and executed by hardware.

Assessment: DIRECT support for conventional electronic binary computers. Preserve the scope: the claim should not imply all computational architectures, including quantum computing, use ordinary binary bits. Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Computer Science §5.1, “Data Storage Inside a Computer” and “Hardware Organization of Systems”.

## 4. Constitutional systems — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: OpenStax, Introduction to Political Science, Chapter 10.

### CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-A
Claim: “In a presidential system, the head of the executive is elected separately from the legislature and there is separation of powers between them.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §9.2 describes presidential systems as having a stronger separation of powers between legislative and executive branches.
- §10.1 states that in presidential regimes the head of government is popularly elected to a fixed term and does not depend on the legislature for power.

Assessment: DIRECT support for the introductory model. Preserve the democratic-regime scope; constitutional arrangements and actual powers vary by country.

Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Political Science §§9.2 and 10.1.

### CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-B
Claim: “In a parliamentary system, the head of the executive is selected by the legislature and is a member of that body.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §10 Key Terms defines parliamentary regimes as systems in which the chief executive is selected by the legislative body and is a member of that legislature.
- §10.1 and §9.2 explain the legislative selection of the prime minister and the close relationship between executive and legislative branches.

Assessment: DIRECT support. Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Political Science §10 Key Terms, “parliamentary regimes”; §§9.2 and 10.1.

### CLM-CONSTITUTIONAL_SYSTEMS_BASICS-C
Claim: “In a semi-presidential system, executive power is divided between a president and a prime minister.”

Exact support:
- OpenStax §10.1 states that semi-presidential regimes have a popularly elected head of state and a legislature-selected head of government, and that both presidents and prime ministers wield political power.
- §10.5 explains that the two offices share authority, with considerable variation among countries.

Assessment: DIRECT support at the general level. Keep the qualification that the allocation of powers varies substantially between semi-presidential constitutions; do not imply a uniform division. Proposed locator: OpenStax Introduction to Political Science §§10.1 and 10.5.

## 5. Construction project management — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: Whole Building Design Guide (WBDG).

### CLM-CONSTRUCTION_PROJECT_MANAGEMENT_BASICS-C
Claim: “A specific management plan depends on project scale, contract, participants and jurisdiction.”

Exact support:
- WBDG “Determining Project Performance Requirements” says project requirements and documentation depend on project purpose, use, size, location, scope, complexity and budget; it also describes owner, design and commissioning team roles.
- WBDG “Utilize Cost and Value Engineering Throughout the Project Life Cycle” explains that cost-management approaches vary with funding source, building type, project goals and client expectations, and that scope, quality, budget and procurement/delivery method must be aligned.
- WBDG commissioning guidance describes how contracts, project delivery mechanisms, team responsibilities and project-specific requirements affect the plan.

Assessment: PARTIAL. WBDG supports project scale, scope, budget, team/participant roles and contract/delivery approach. It does not establish the full jurisdiction-specific legal requirements. Add applicable local building, procurement, construction and contract law for any jurisdiction-specific use. The current canonical Source is only the WBDG landing page; replace the generic EU description with precise page locators and add specific WBDG pages as separate sources.

Proposed locators:
- WBDG, “Determining Project Performance Requirements”, Introduction and “Approach”.
- WBDG, “Utilize Cost and Value Engineering Throughout the Project Life Cycle”, Overview and Planning Phase.
- WBDG, “Roles and Responsibilities in the Commissioning Process”, “Structuring the Commissioning Team” and “Commissioning Project Management”.

## 6. Development economics — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: World Bank Economic Review, “What Is Considered Development Economics? Commonalities and Differences in University Courses around the Developing World”.

### CLM-DEVELOPMENT_ECONOMICS_BASICS-A
Claim: “Development economics studies issues related to economic growth, poverty and inequality, human capital and institutions.”

Exact support:
- The World Bank Economic Review study analyzes the topics taught in development-economics courses across 124 undergraduate courses in 46 developing countries and 70 master's courses in 29 developing countries, with comparison courses in the United States.
- It identifies growth theory, poverty and inequality, human capital and institutions as the four core topics taught in at least half of the courses studied.
- The study also emphasizes variation in how development economics is conceptualized across countries and courses.

Assessment: DIRECT support. Preserve the nuance that this is a study of course coverage and the field's common core, not an exhaustive or universally agreed definition. Proposed locator: McKenzie, “What Is Considered Development Economics?”, abstract and findings on the four-topic core.

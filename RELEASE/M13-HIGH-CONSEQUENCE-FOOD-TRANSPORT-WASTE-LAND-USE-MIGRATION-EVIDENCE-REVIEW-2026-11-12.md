## 1. Food security — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-FOOD_SECURITY_BASICS`, FAO, *Handbook for Defining and Setting up a Food Security Information and Early Warning System (FSIEWS)*.

### Claim A — `CLM-FOOD_SECURITY_BASICS-A`

Claim: food security includes physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food.

Exact support: FAO handbook, Chapter 1, §1.4 “Definition of Food Security”, quotes the unanimously adopted 1996 World Food Summit definition: all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food for an active and healthy life.

Proposed locator: FAO FSIEWS Handbook, Chapter 1, §1.4, “Definition of Food Security”, paragraph 2.

Assessment: direct support, nearly verbatim.

### Claim B — `CLM-FOOD_SECURITY_BASICS-B`

Claim: food-security assessment considers availability and stability of supplies as well as access.

Exact support: the same FAO §1.4 identifies availability of staple foods, stability of supplies and access for all as the classic dimensions; it adds biological utilization as another dimension in the World Food Summit definition.

Proposed locator: FAO FSIEWS Handbook, Chapter 1, §1.4, paragraph 3.

Assessment: direct support. Preserve the broader four-dimension model (availability, access, utilization and stability) where the material explains food security in full.

### Claim C — `CLM-FOOD_SECURITY_BASICS-C`

Claim: information systems and early warning are used to understand food-security conditions and support decisions.

Exact support:
- FAO FSIEWS Handbook, Foreword, states the need to improve information available to governments, international partners and civil society to guide action against food insecurity.
- The Foreword explains that the Food Insecurity and Vulnerability Information and Mapping Systems (FIVIMS) initiative was undertaken to improve mobilization and utilization of information needed to make decisions on food security.
- Chapter 1, §1.4 says that a detailed country analysis is required to define and set up a monitoring system.

Proposed locator: FAO FSIEWS Handbook, Foreword, paragraphs 2–3; Chapter 1, §1.4, opening paragraph.

Assessment: direct support.

## 2. Transportation infrastructure — 3 Claims

Canonical Source: `SRC-TRANSPORTATION_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS`, FHWA, “What Is Transportation Asset Management?”, https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/asset/if08008/amo_02.cfm

### Claim A — `CLM-TRANSPORTATION_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS-A`

Claim: transport infrastructure includes physical assets that enable movement of people and goods.

Exact support:
- FHWA lines 33–34 describes public transportation assets as roads, bridges and other facilities.
- The same page identifies pavements, bridges and other roadway/roadside features as transportation infrastructure assets.
- The broader page includes facilities and network performance/availability.

Proposed locator: FHWA, “What Is Transportation Asset Management?”, paragraph beginning “For transportation agencies…” (lines 33–34) and §“Defining Transportation Asset Management”.

Assessment: direct support for the physical-asset definition. If “people and goods” is intended as a separate functional definition, add a transportation-system definition from FHWA/UN ESCAP.

### Claim B — `CLM-TRANSPORTATION_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS-B`

Claim: asset management links infrastructure condition, performance and decisions about resources.

Exact support:
- FHWA lines 37–40 states that asset management links user expectations for condition, performance and availability with management/investment strategies and that decisions use data on resources, current condition/performance and future performance estimates.
- Lines 42–46 list policy-driven resource allocation, performance measures, option/trade-off analysis, quality information and monitoring/accountability.

Proposed locator: FHWA, “Defining Transportation Asset Management”, paragraphs 2–4 and “Core Principles of Asset Management”.

Assessment: direct support.

### Claim C — `CLM-TRANSPORTATION_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS-C`

Claim: infrastructure assessment depends on goals, data, risks and time horizon.

Exact support:
- FHWA lines 37–40 says a decision framework is guided by performance goals, covers an extended time horizon, draws on economics/engineering and uses data on current condition and estimated future performance.
- Lines 45–46 require credible/current information and performance monitoring.
- Lines 66–67 says the amount of information required depends on agency goals and willingness to take risks and that assurance must be balanced against cost.

Proposed locator: FHWA, “Defining Transportation Asset Management”, lines 37–46 and final paragraph, lines 66–67.

Assessment: direct support within U.S. transportation asset management. Do not treat the FHWA model as a universal statutory standard for every country or infrastructure class.

## 3. Waste infrastructure — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-WASTE_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS`, U.S. EPA, Sustainable Materials Management, https://www.epa.gov/smm

### Claim A — `CLM-WASTE_INFRASTRUCTURE_BASICS-A`

Claim: a waste-management system includes collection, transport, processing and disposal or resource recovery.

Exact support:
- EPA “Municipal Solid Waste Landfills”, “Municipal Solid Waste Transfer Stations”, states that waste is unloaded from collection vehicles, held, reloaded onto larger long-distance transport vehicles (trains, trucks, barges) and shipped to landfills or treatment/disposal facilities.
- EPA “Non-Hazardous Materials and Waste Management Hierarchy”, “Recycling and Composting”, defines recycling as collection, sorting, processing and remanufacturing.
- The same EPA hierarchy page, “Energy Recovery” and “Treatment and Disposal”, describes recovery to heat/electricity/fuel, physical/chemical/biological treatment and landfills.
- EPA Sustainable Materials Management page frames material/waste management across the life cycle.

Proposed locators: EPA Municipal Solid Waste Landfills, “Municipal Solid Waste Transfer Stations”, paragraphs 1–2; EPA Non-Hazardous Materials and Waste Management Hierarchy, “Recycling and Composting”, “Energy Recovery”, and “Treatment and Disposal”.

Assessment: direct support for the system stages. The canonical SMM homepage is too broad to support each stage by itself; add the two specific EPA pages as separate Source/Evidence Use records. These sources are U.S. non-hazardous municipal waste examples and must not be generalized to hazardous, medical, industrial or radioactive waste without separate standards.

## 4. Land use planning — 1 Claim

Canonical Source: `SRC-LAND_USE_PLANNING_BASICS`, UN-Habitat, “Land”, https://unhabitat.org/topic/land

### Claim B — `CLM-LAND_USE_PLANNING_BASICS-B`

Claim: planning decisions influence access to infrastructure, services and transport connections.

Exact support:
- Habitat Agenda (1996), Chapter IV.C, paragraph 109, says access to land in rapidly growing urban areas is affected by competing demands for housing, industry, commerce, infrastructure, transport, agriculture, open space and green areas.
- The paragraph also notes that unsuitable location can expose residents to economic, environmental and health risks, and that urban development must be brought into harmony with the natural environment and settlement system.
- UN-Habitat's Land topic page identifies land-use planning, land governance and access to land as core issues.

Proposed locator: Habitat Agenda, Chapter IV.C, “Sustainable land use”, paragraph 109; add the Habitat Agenda as a separate primary Source/Evidence Use rather than relying only on the UN-Habitat topic landing page.

Assessment: direct support.

## 5. Migration and mobility — 1 Claim

Canonical Sources:
- `SRC-MIGRATION_AND_MOBILITY_BASICS`, IOM, World Migration Report Educators Toolkit, “What is Migration?”.
- `SRC-MIGRATION_AND_MOBILITY_BASICS-INDEPENDENT`, World Bank, “Migration and Labor Mobility”.

### Claim A — `CLM-MIGRATION_AND_MOBILITY_BASICS-A`

Claim: migration is movement from a place of usual residence to a new residence, within a country or across an international border.

Exact support:
- IOM World Migration Report Educators Toolkit, Module 1, “What is Migration?”, defines migration as movement away from a place of usual residence, across an international border or within a State, to a new residence.
- The same page, “Internal and international migration”, explains the distinction between movement within a country and movement across state boundaries.
- The UN's migrant definition, based on IOM, similarly includes movement across international borders or within a State away from habitual residence.

Source-scope finding:
- The second linked Source, World Bank “Migration and Labor Mobility”, discusses drivers and policy (income gaps, demographics, climate change, conflict, social exclusion and services) but does not define migration as stated in this Claim.
- It is a useful independent institutional source for migration context and drivers, but it is not direct independent corroboration of the definition. The Evidence Use description should not claim that it verifies the definition unless a direct World Bank definition source is added.

Proposed locators: IOM Educators Toolkit, Module 1, opening definition and “Internal and international migration”, paragraphs 1–2; World Bank Migration and Labor Mobility, “Context” and “Main drivers of migration” only for a separate drivers Claim.

Assessment: direct support from IOM; source-role mismatch for the World Bank Evidence Use.

Disposition: HOLD until the World Bank EU role is narrowed or a World Bank source with an explicit migration definition is added.

## 2. Replacement/augmentation sources examined

### Source candidate 1 — IES and NSF, Common Guidelines for Education Research and Development (August 2013)

Official publication:
https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/ies/document/2024/10/Common%20Guidelines%20for%20Education%20Research%20and%20Development%202013.pdf

Relevant pinpoint passages:
- Preface, printed pp. 4–5: the agencies describe education research across STEM and other content areas; the work includes learning, teaching, outcomes, and research types.
- Introduction, printed pp. 7–8: the report describes research generating evidence about strategies and interventions for increasing student learning, including studies of fundamental understanding of education and learning, associations, design/testing, and impact on education-related outcomes.
- Table 1, printed p. 12: foundational research advances knowledge of education and learning, theory, methodology, teaching and learning; early-stage research studies relationships between education/learning outcomes and relevant factors.
- Table 4, printed pp. 18–19: research plans include hypotheses, study design, sampling, data analysis, statistical power for hypothesis testing, qualitative-study sampling rationale, primary/secondary data, and analysis of text, video and observational data.

### Source candidate 2 — Tipton and Olsen, Enhancing the Generalizability of Impact Studies in Education (IES, NCEE 2022-003, February 2022)

Official publication:
https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/migrated/nces_pubs/ncee/pubs/2022003/pdf/2022003.pdf

The publication explicitly states that it is in the public domain (front matter, printed p. ii; PDF lines 32–37) and supplies the recommended citation.

Relevant pinpoint passages:
- Printed pp. 1–3: findings from a study sample do not automatically generalize to other schools/populations; effects can vary across students and schools; target populations and moderators must be defined.
- Printed pp. 34–36: assess similarity between the sample and target population, compare observed potential moderators, and examine threats from unobserved moderators.
- Printed pp. 41–43: adjust for differences, redefine the target population where necessary, or restrict inferences to the study sample.

The guide is specifically about impact studies in education, especially prospective multi-site evaluations. It supports the transferability boundary at that scope; do not universalize its sampling prescriptions to every form of educational inquiry.

## 3. Claim-by-claim reassessment

### Claim A — learning processes and student outcomes

Claim: “Исследования образования изучают процессы обучения и результаты учащихся.”

**Assessment: DIRECT at the introductory scope.**
- Common Guidelines, Introduction, printed pp. 7–8, describes research intended to generate evidence about strategies and interventions for increasing student learning and includes research on fundamental understanding of education and learning.
- Table 1, printed p. 12, expressly describes foundational knowledge about teaching and/or learning and research relating education/learning outcomes to relevant factors.

The source is a U.S. federal interagency research framework, not a universal definition of every education-research tradition. The Russian Claim is broad and should remain a high-level description rather than a formal taxonomy.

**Proposed locator:** Common Guidelines, Introduction, printed pp. 7–8; Table 1, printed p. 12.

### Claim B — quantitative and qualitative methods

Claim: “Образовательные исследования используют количественные и качественные методы для проверки вопросов об обучении.”

**Assessment: DIRECT, with method-scope precision.**
- Common Guidelines, Table 4, printed pp. 18–19, requires hypothesis-testing proposals to address statistical power and relevant effect/relationship sizes; it separately requires qualitative studies/components to justify sample size and selection.
- The same table requires plans for statistical models and procedures for analysis of text, video and observational data.
- The report also distinguishes research designs and methods according to the questions and evidence expected.

This supports the existence and use of quantitative and qualitative approaches in education research. It does not establish that every study must use both methods or that mixed-methods designs are always superior.

**Proposed locator:** Common Guidelines, Table 4, printed pp. 18–19; see also Table 1, printed p. 12.

### Claim C — comparability when transferring findings

Claim: “Перенос результата исследования в другой образовательный контекст требует проверки сопоставимости условий.”

**Assessment: DIRECT for generalizability of impact findings; narrow the implied universality.**
- IES NCEE 2022-003, printed pp. 1–3, explains why findings from participating schools cannot simply be assumed to apply to schools outside the study and identifies variation in students, schools and intervention effects.
- Recommendation 5, printed pp. 34–36, directs researchers to assess similarity between the sample and target population, compare observed moderators and consider unobserved moderators.
- Recommendation 6, printed pp. 41–43, describes adjustment, redefining the target population or restricting inferences to the sample.

The source directly supports checking comparability before generalizing impact findings. The Claim's current wording says “result of research” generally; the cited guide is specifically about impact studies. Either preserve this boundary in the EU or narrow the Claim to “перенос результатов оценки воздействия образовательного вмешательства”.

**Proposed locator:** IES NCEE 2022-003, printed pp. 1–3, Recommendations 5–6, printed pp. 34–43.
